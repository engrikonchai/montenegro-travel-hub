Montenegrin food doesn't get talked about the way Italian or Greek food does, which is a little strange once you've actually eaten your way through the country. Order right and you'll get smoked ham cured on a mountainside, squid-ink risotto pulled from the same water you can see from your table, and a red wine that literally doesn't exist anywhere else in the world.

## The mountains: pršut, cheese, and kačamak

Start with Njeguški pršut, the country's most celebrated food export. It comes from Njeguši, a village of well under a hundred people on the slopes of Mount Lovćen, about 30 minutes by car from Kotor on a genuinely white-knuckle serpentine road. The hind legs are salted for around three weeks, air-dried for another three, then cold-smoked over beechwood for four months or longer. The altitude and the mix of mountain air and sea humidity are what locals credit for the flavor — you'll see it served everywhere, but the platters sold directly from Njeguši households, alongside the local sheep's cheese, are the real thing.

Order njeguški steak in a restaurant and you're getting a pork or veal schnitzel rolled around that same ham and a local cream cheese called kajmak, then breaded and fried. It's rich, a little indulgent, and it shows up on nearly every traditional menu on the coast and inland alike.

Kačamak is the humbler counterpart: a thick porridge of cornmeal and mashed potato, finished with kajmak and a spoon of sour cream or yogurt. It's simple peasant food by design, and it's exactly what you want after a long hike or a cold ferry crossing. In the mountains you'll also find ispod sača — meat and potatoes slow-cooked for hours under a domed metal lid buried in hot coals, usually lamb or veal, falling-apart tender by the time it's served.

## The coast: buzara, black risotto, and fish by the kilo

Head down to the water and the menu changes completely. Buzara is the dish to know — mussels, clams, or prawns stewed in garlic, olive oil, and white wine, mopped up with bread. Crni rižoto, black risotto made with cuttlefish ink, is the other coastal signature; it's dense and briny, and a good way to judge whether a kitchen actually knows what it's doing with seafood.

Whole fish — brancin (sea bass) or orada (sea bream) — is typically priced by weight and grilled simply with olive oil and Swiss chard on the side, so confirm the price before you order if the menu doesn't spell it out. Octopus salad, dressed simply in garlic, parsley, and lemon, is a good lighter option if you've already had a heavy lunch.

## What to drink with it

Montenegro has its own wine identity, centered on the Crmnica region around Lake Skadar and the town of Virpazar. Vranac is the local red — deep-colored, full-bodied, with blackberry and blueberry notes — and it's been grown in the Skadar Lake valley since at least the 14th century. Pair it with red meat, aged cheese, or the pršut itself; locals do exactly that. Krstač is the white counterpart, grown nowhere else in the world, with a lighter, appley character that works well alongside seafood.

Plantaže, the state-owned winery outside Podgorica, produces the vast majority of the country's wine and runs tastings at its Šipčanik cellar, a former military hangar converted into a wine cave, where a standard one-hour tasting covers all three of the country's main native grapes. If you'd rather see something smaller, the family-run wineries around Virpazar offer tastings by request, usually paired with cheese, cured meats, and olives from their own land — a nice way to spend an afternoon if you're already out near Lake Skadar, and a good excuse to combine a wine stop with a boat trip on the lake itself.

## Street food and something sweet

Burek is the everyday staple — a layered phyllo pastry filled with cheese, meat, or spinach, sold at pretty much every bakery in the country for €1.50-2.50 and eaten standing up, often for breakfast. Ćevapi, small grilled meat patties served with flatbread and a chunky tomato-cucumber salad, are the closest thing to Montenegrin fast food and a reliable, cheap option almost anywhere.

For dessert, krempita is the one to know — a custard cream cake with layers of puff pastry, lighter than a lot of Balkan sweets and less cloying than baklava, which also shows up on most menus if you want the more classic option. Either pairs well with the strong Turkish-style coffee that follows most meals here.

## Where to actually eat this stuff

The word to look for is konoba — a small, usually family-run tavern, as opposed to a formal restaurant. Prices climb fast in the old-town cores of Kotor and Budva, where you're paying partly for the view and partly for cruise-ship foot traffic; walk a few streets back from the water and the same dishes tend to run noticeably cheaper without any drop in quality. As a rough guide: ćevapi at a grill spot runs €6-10, and a proper seaside seafood dinner with wine lands somewhere around €30-50 per person. Cash is still preferred at a lot of smaller places, so carry some even if a card machine is visible on the counter.

If you're driving yourself between the coast and Njeguši or out to the wine country near Virpazar, having your own car makes it much easier to string a few of these stops into one day — the mountain road up to Njeguši in particular isn't something you want to be timing around a tour bus schedule.

## Bottom line

Eat pršut and kajmak in the mountains, buzara and black risotto on the coast, and don't skip a glass of Vranac with either. Look for the word konoba over anything glossier, and don't be shy about walking a block away from the water to find the better-value version of the same meal.
